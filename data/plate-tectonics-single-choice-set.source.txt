Plate tectonics is the scientific theory that Earth's outer shell is divided into large slabs of solid rock, called plates, that glide slowly over the mantle beneath them. The theory explains how continents drift, why earthquakes and volcanoes cluster in certain regions, and how mountain ranges and ocean basins form over millions of years. Earth's outermost layers are the crust and the upper mantle, which together form the rigid lithosphere. The lithosphere is broken into roughly fifteen major plates and many smaller ones. Beneath it lies the asthenosphere, a hotter, weaker layer that behaves like a very slow-moving fluid. Heat rising from Earth's interior sets up convection currents in the mantle, and these currents, along with the pull of dense sinking slabs, drag the plates across the surface at speeds of a few centimetres per year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Plate boundaries are where most geological action happens, and there are three main types. At divergent boundaries, plates move apart. Magma rises to fill the gap and cools to form new crust, as happens along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where the seafloor is slowly spreading. At convergent boundaries, plates move toward each other. When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate sinks beneath the other in a process called subduction, forming deep ocean trenches, volcanic arcs, and powerful earthquakes. When two continental plates collide, neither sinks easily, and the crust buckles upward to build mountain ranges such as the Himalayas. At transform boundaries, plates slide past one another horizontally. The San Andreas Fault in California is a well-known example, and the friction between the plates there produces frequent earthquakes. The evidence for plate tectonics is broad. The coastlines of South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces, and matching rock formations and fossils are found on both continents. Studies of the ocean floor reveal symmetrical magnetic stripes on either side of mid-ocean ridges, recording reversals of Earth's magnetic field as new crust formed and spread outward. The global distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes traces the outlines of the plates almost exactly, and satellite measurements now record plate motion directly. Plate tectonics also shapes the planet over long timescales. Around 300 million years ago the continents were joined in a single supercontinent called Pangaea, which later broke apart into the continents we recognize today. This cycle of assembly and breakup has happened several times in Earth's history and will continue. The movement of plates influences climate, sea level, the distribution of natural resources, and the evolution of life by repeatedly connecting and isolating populations of organisms. Understanding plate tectonics helps scientists assess hazards. Regions near active boundaries face greater risk from earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions, and this knowledge guides building codes, land use planning, and early warning systems.
